Izere/Methodology
Technical design · draft · October 2026

How Izere is designed to work, and how you can check it.

This page describes the approach Izere is being built around: the model, the features, the explanations and the audit trail. It is a design, not a record of results. Izere is at prototype stage and has not yet been tested on historical programmes.

§ 01

Problem framing

EU-funded programmes are delivered through a web of contracting authorities, prime contractors, subcontractors, regional managing authorities and sub-recipients. The signals that show whether delivery is on track are spread across procurement notices, milestone reports, governance changes and disbursement events. No single one of them predicts execution risk on its own.

Oversight tends to treat programmes as independent objects with linear indicators, and relies heavily on retrospective review. A programme is often flagged well after problems have started.

Execution risk is structural. It sits in the relationships between actors, not in any one actor's reporting line.

Izere treats this as graph-structured prediction. A programme is modelled as a graph of actors and dependencies that changes over time, and risk is read from the shape of that graph. The hypothesis is that this picks up trouble earlier than linear indicators do. That hypothesis has not been tested yet.

§ 02

Model class

The planned risk model is a heterogeneous graph neural network (a GraphSAGE base with attention-based message passing), trained on procurement, governance and financial-execution graphs. The graph has different kinds of nodes and edges, such as contracting authorities, suppliers, sub-recipients and dependencies, and each kind carries its own features.

Why a graph model and not a tabular one

Tabular models capture the risk of a single actor well. They find it harder to represent structural risk: bidder concentration, governance distance, and fragility in supplier dependency chains. A graph model is meant to capture these. Whether it does better in practice is what the evaluation plan below is for.

# layer-wise message passing
h_v^(k+1) = σ( W^(k) · concat(
  h_v^(k),
  AGG_{u ∈ N(v)}( att(h_v^(k), h_u^(k)) · h_u^(k) )
) )

# risk score = sigmoid(MLP(pooled_graph_embedding))

Current status

No trained model exists yet. The architecture above is the starting design. Model size and training settings will be chosen during development and published with the first evaluation.

§ 03

Feature definitions

Features are meant to be derived deterministically from public and institutional data sources (see Platform → Sources). Each one will have a published formula and a reference implementation, so an auditor can recompute it from raw inputs.

§ 04

Training protocol

The design uses federated learning across institutional deployments. Raw data stays inside each institution. Each deployment computes updates locally, encrypts them, and contributes to a shared model through secure aggregation.

Why federated

Two reasons. First, it reduces how much sensitive and personal data has to be shared, which makes data-protection review simpler. Second, institutions can benefit from patterns seen elsewhere without negotiating data-sharing agreements that can take years.

Governance

Retraining and drift monitoring will run on a regular schedule. No model goes into production without sign-off from a designated reviewer at each participating institution.

§ 05

Explainability

Every score is meant to break down into the features that drove it. Izere uses SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations), a widely used method for attributing a model's output to its inputs.

The attribution is computed over the full graph context, not only the features of one node. A programme's score can therefore show contributions from neighbouring actors and dependencies, which makes structural risk visible and open to challenge.

No score is shown without an evidence chain that an auditor can read from start to finish, and challenge.

Each score is stored with its full breakdown and a link back to the source record. An auditor looking at a flagged programme can follow any contribution back to the contract notice, governance event or financial signal behind it.

§ 06

Evaluation plan

Izere has not been evaluated yet. The plan is to test it on past EU programmes (CEF, Cohesion, RRF) from the 2014–2020 cycle. Ground truth would be the delivery outcomes recorded in ECA and OECD reports, and predictions would use only information available 12 months before the problem was officially flagged.

We would compare it against a simple linear baseline and against indicator-based approaches such as Arachne-style risk flags, and report:

· AUC-ROC for delays of more than six months
· Recall at a fixed precision
· Lead time before the official flag
· False positive rate
· Calibration error

We will publish the results here, including disappointing ones, before asking any institution to rely on a score.

§ 07

Audit pathway

The design gives independent auditors, including institutional internal-audit teams, read access to:

· Versioned model cards and training-run metadata
· The full feature derivation pipeline with reference implementations
· The SHAP breakdown for every score, linked to the raw input
· An append-only log of data ingestion and model decisions
· Documentation prepared with the EU AI Act in mind

The aim is that any score, at any point in time, can be reproduced from raw inputs. That is the first design constraint, not something to add later to satisfy regulation.

§ 08

Limitations

These are the limits we expect, and we will test each one. Performance is likely to be weaker on small programmes (under roughly €5M), where there is little signal; in Member States with limited procurement transparency; and on early-stage RRF measures, where milestone definitions are still being negotiated.

Izere is a decision-support tool, not a decision-maker. Scores inform institutional judgement and do not replace it. No automated action should follow from a score without human sign-off.

If you see a weakness in this approach, we want to hear about it.
