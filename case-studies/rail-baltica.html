Rail Baltica: what earlier warning could look like.

Rail Baltica is the largest cross-border rail project in the EU. In January 2026 the European Court of Auditors put the cost of the first phase at around €24 billion and said it will miss its 2030 deadline. This page explains which signals Izere is designed to read on a programme like this. It is not a back-test. We have not yet run a model on Rail Baltica.

€23.8B

2030
Original deadline, now expected to be missed

In January 2026 the European Court of Auditors reported that the first phase of Rail Baltica will cost far more than planned and will not be finished by 2030. The final completion date is uncertain.

Projects of this size rarely go wrong in a single step. Costs, schedules and governance drift apart over years, across three countries, many contracting authorities and a long chain of contractors. The early signs are scattered through procurement notices, progress reports and decisions, and none of them is conclusive on its own.

That is the situation Izere is designed for: reading many weak signals together, and showing which of them are driving a programme's risk score.

Why programmes like this are hard to monitor.

Most supervision relies on programmes reporting their own progress against fixed indicators. A milestone is either met or missed. By the time one is formally missed, the underlying problems have usually been building for some time.

Audit institutions such as the ECA review what has happened, and that is their job. Izere is meant to complement that work by watching for signs of trouble while there is still time to act.

Linear monitoring asks whether a milestone slipped. Graph monitoring asks whether the structure that produces milestones is still holding.
Signals

Four signals Izere is designed to read.

These describe the planned features (see Methodology § 03). They are not findings about Rail Baltica.
F.001 · Bidder concentrationPlanned

Few bidders, concentrated awards.

A shrinking pool of qualified bidders, or a few firms winning most of the awards, can point to a fragile supply side. Izere would measure this from public procurement notices on TED.

F.003 · Coordination cadencePlanned

Slower coordination between countries.

On a cross-border project, fewer joint meetings and slower document flow between the authorities involved may signal that decisions are getting harder. This depends on what programmes publish.

F.004 · Milestone amendmentsPlanned

Milestones and baselines that keep changing.

Repeated amendments to milestones, budgets or contracts can show that the original plan is no longer holding, even when no single change is announced as a reset.

F.010 · Co-financing gapPlanned

Funding for later phases not confirmed.

A gap between announced and confirmed national co-financing for upcoming phases is a classic early sign of delay. Izere would compare the two over time.

Testing the idea

How we plan to test it on Rail Baltica.

The fair test is a back-test: rebuild the programme from public records as they stood at each date, run the model, and compare the timing of its flags with the ECA's findings.

We have not done this yet. When we do, we will publish the method and the results, including if the model finds nothing useful. Until then, any claim about how early Izere would have spotted Rail Baltica's problems would be a guess.

What a fair back-test needs

Dated data. Contract notices, programme reports and public documents, each with the date it became public.

No hindsight. At each date the model sees only what was public at that date.

A baseline. A simple indicator-based approach to compare against.

Open method. The feature definitions and pipeline published so others can repeat it.

What we would like to do next.

Run the Rail Baltica back-test, then repeat it on other large CEF projects. If you work on a programme or an audit team and would like to help us design a fair test, or challenge how we plan to do it, we would like to hear from you.
